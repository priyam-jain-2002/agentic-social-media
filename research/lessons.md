# Lessons from posts and comparative research

**Evidence boundary:** These are user-observed performance signals and editorial hypotheses, not proof that a platform algorithm rewarded or punished a particular technique.

## Observed content pattern
- Actual project/event/practical developer-tool posts on the user's LinkedIn previously drew more impressions than some recent abstract market-thesis posts. Post dates, networks, audiences and formats differ.
- Sales masterclass → AI portfolio interpretation: readers praised the wording but engagement was low. The interesting original test arrived late.
- CUDA, US controls and India: user reported roughly 300 impressions and two engagements. The framing was speculative, heavy on context and light on firsthand proof; alternative explanations include distribution, topic relevance, timing and network behavior.
- Headless commerce: a concrete firsthand builder use case offers a clearer benefit to frontend engineers than a generic AI infrastructure forecast, **if** real artifact and technical boundaries are shown.

## Editorial changes adopted
1. Reader promise in the first two lines, not a preamble about where the idea arose.
2. Deliver the claim early and proof soon after. No suspense staircase.
3. Match content pillar and reader persona before selecting platform.
4. Real screenshot/demo/repository beats speculative illustration for claims about work actually built.
5. Tags and mentions are metadata / attribution decisions, not a substitute for the above.
6. No universal word-count, hashtag or timing 'algorithm rule' is inferred from isolated outcomes.
7. Record comparative performance at matched 24h, 72h, 7d windows; distinguish impressions from engagement and from qualified audience impact.

## Research limitations / backlog
The repository does not yet contain a rigorous matched sample of similar small LinkedIn/X/blog creators, including unsuccessful posts. A sample should record audience size, content age, reach if visible, engagement type, hook, length, proof type, format and citations. Without it, numerical recommendations are editorial experiments, not validated effect sizes.
